### Accessibility

Our research is primarily focused around accessibility and inclusion, with all of our
members having deep understanding and expertise with disabilities, many with lived
experiences. With a diverse team of Disabled co-researchers, we live by the motto,
“Nothing about us without us”, and people with lived-experience of disability will be
advising, refining and perfecting our installation. We pride ourselves on making
everybody feel welcome and part of our community, and will be happy to help make
the Royal Society and all the other exhibits more inclusive and accessible too!
One of the most fantastic aspects of our exhibition is that it is not only accessible, but
also helps visitors understand accessibility itself! The city has been intentionally
designed with barriers that mirror many of those encountered in everyday life. As
visitors modify the environment to improve their robot's experience, they engage with
a powerful metaphor for accessibility and inclusion, helping to communicate the
impact of disability in a simple, relatable, and memorable way.
Visitors choose their robot's capabilities and personalise its body using arts and crafts
materials before placing it within the city. As the robot encounters environmental
barriers, visitors observe how its experience is shaped not by its characteristics alone,
but by the design of its surroundings. Through repeated cycles of observation and
adaptation, the city is modified until those barriers are removed. This process allows
visitors to witness first-hand how changing environments, rather than individuals, can
transform outcomes. The robots provide a clear and engaging illustration of

accessibility in action, while disabled co-researchers ground the experience in real-
world perspectives and lived experience.

We always have many approaches to consider:
Mobility: All interactive elements are multimodal with a broad variety of control types
(from buttons to voice control) and will be positioned for seated and standing use, with
wheelchair access and clear space around the exhibit. Staff will support participation
according to each visitor’s preferences.
Vision: We will use high-contrast graphics, large-print instructions and tactile features.
Spoken descriptions, signage and staff-guided exploration will tell the story, explain
the objective and guide visitors through the challenges. Activities will avoid relying
solely on colour, shape or small visual details.
Hearing: Instructions and key messages will be available in written and visual formats,
with captions for video content. We will have a hearing loop built in. All interactions
and robot actions cause visual cues as well as musical changes. We are hoping to
bring haptic blankets and vests that vibrate so that music can also be felt.
Learning & sensory access: We will provide plain-language instructions, pictures,
short demonstrations and activities with different levels of complexity, many without
direct objectives or set ways to complete or achieve. Visitors can explore and
experiment at their own pace, observe before participating and choose whether to
engage with sound. We will avoid flashing effects.

We will provide a quieter session at preset times with headsets.
Our aim is to offer several ways to understand and contribute to each activity.
Accessibility testing will inform the final layout and interactions alongside consultation
with the Royal Society about venue access and available support.
We are keen to educate and change the world.
